## Scope

The scholarship behind *how* the APS collects, not *what* it collects. Four threads:

1. **The experience-centered approach** — the methodological core. Hufford's argument that anomalous belief can follow from widely shared experiential events, rather than only from tradition or pathology, is the premise the intake instrument is built on.
2. **The project's own documented lineage** — the Grimms on linguistic fidelity, the SPR on pattern-through-scale. Both are named as the project's descent in `APS_Elevator_Pitches.md` §4; this file is where that claim is made answerable.
3. **Genre and transmission** — memorate, legend, ostension, vernacular expression. What kind of object a witness account *is*.
4. **Research ethics** — obligations that attach to collecting and publishing other people's accounts.

## Boundary notes

Works here are cited for their **method**, not their subject. Hufford 1982 is about sleep paralysis but sits here because the project borrowed its procedure; the same volume is required reading for 💭 Type IV and 👁 Type VI, where it is cross-referenced rather than duplicated.

A work moves out of this file when its value to an analyst is what it says about a phenomenon rather than about procedure.

## Reading order

New regional unit managers: **Hufford 1982**, then **Honko 1964**, then **Smith 2021**. That sequence gives the method, the genre vocabulary, and the ethical frame in roughly 700 pages.

## The experience-centered approach

### Hufford, David J. — *The Terror That Comes in the Night: An Experience-Centered Study of Supernatural Assault Traditions*

Philadelphia: University of Pennsylvania Press, 1982. ISBN 9780812213058. OCLC [7977692](https://www.worldcat.org/oclc/7977692).

Hufford analyses night-assault narratives and argues that the experiences generating them are stable across cultures that lack any shared tradition to transmit — which makes the "cultural source hypothesis" insufficient on its own. The methodological move the APS inherits is narrower than the famous conclusion: *collect the phenomenology first, in the witness's own terms, and defer explanation.* The intake instrument's refusal to ask leading questions descends directly from this.

`CORE` · `frame: method` · cross-ref → 💭 IV, 👁 VI

### Hufford, David J. — "Beings Without Bodies: An Experience-Centered Theory of the Belief in Spirits"

In Barbara Walker, ed., *Out of the Ordinary: Folklore and the Supernatural*, 11–45. Logan: Utah State University Press, 1995. Volume ISBN 9780874211962.

The explicit statement of the theory that *Terror* demonstrated in a single case. Hufford generalises from night assault to entity experience broadly, and sets out what an experience-centered account owes the witness. Shorter and more directly usable as a procedural reference than the 1982 monograph.

`CORE` · `frame: method` · cross-ref → 👁 VI

### Bennett, Gillian — *Alas, Poor Ghost!: Traditions of Belief in Story and Discourse*

Logan: Utah State University Press, 1999. ISBN 9780874212778 (pbk.).

Bennett interviews women about experiences of the dead and attends closely to *how they talk* — the hedges, disclaimers, and negotiations of credibility that surround a told account. The most useful single work for anyone conducting a debriefing, because it shows that a witness's apparent uncertainty is often a social performance rather than a measure of conviction. Pairs directly with `APS_Debriefing_Protocol_Training.docx`.

`CORE` · `frame: method` · cross-ref → 👁 VI

## Documented lineage

### Grimm, Jacob, and Wilhelm Grimm — *The Original Folk and Fairy Tales of the Brothers Grimm: The Complete First Edition*

Translated and edited by Jack Zipes; illustrated by Andrea Dezsö. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press, 2014. ISBN 9780691160597. xiii + 519 pp.

The 1812–1815 first edition, before the Grimms' successive revisions smoothed the tales. Cited here because the project claims descent from the Grimms' stated commitment to recording the vernacular as spoken. Zipes's edition is the right one for that claim precisely because it shows the tales at their least edited.

`KEY` · `frame: method`

### Ellis, John M. — *One Fairy Story Too Many: The Brothers Grimm and Their Tales*

Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1983. ISBN-10 0226205460. OCLC [9217392](https://www.worldcat.org/oclc/9217392). ix + 214 pp.

Ellis demonstrates that the Grimms edited heavily, invented, combined sources, and overstated the fidelity they claimed in their prefaces. **Required reading alongside the Grimms, not optional.** A project citing the Grimms as a fidelity model without engaging this critique would be making a claim its own bibliography does not support, and an academic reader will know the literature. The honest position is that the APS aspires to a standard the Grimms announced rather than met — which is a better story anyway, and one the intake architecture can actually evidence.

`CORE` · `frame: method`

### Sidgwick, Henry, Alice Johnson, Frederic W. H. Myers, Frank Podmore, and Eleanor Mildred Sidgwick — "Report on the Census of Hallucinations"

*Proceedings of the Society for Psychical Research* 10 (1894): 25–422.

Some 17,000 responses to a single question about waking impressions of a presence or voice, analysed for base rates and co-occurrence. The direct ancestor of the APS registry: find pattern through scale rather than through the strength of any one case, and publish the aggregate so others can re-analyse it. The report is also unusually frank about its own sampling problems, which is worth reading for its own sake.

**Dating note:** the census was conducted beginning in 1889; the report is 1894. `APS_Elevator_Pitches.md` §4 refers to "the SPR's 1889 Census of Hallucinations" — accurate as to the census, not the publication. Cite 1894 in written work.

`CORE` · `frame: method`

### Gurney, Edmund, Frederic W. H. Myers, and Frank Podmore — *Phantasms of the Living*

2 vols. London: published at the Rooms of the Society for Psychical Research and Trübner and Co., 1886. Pre-ISBN. [Vol. I](https://archive.org/details/phantasmsoflivin001gurn) · [Vol. II](https://archive.org/details/phantasmsoflivin02gurn)

The case-collection method before the census refined it: several hundred accounts gathered with corroborating correspondence, dates of first telling, and named witnesses. Of continuing operational interest for its evidentiary apparatus — the authors' concern with *when an account was first told, and to whom* anticipates the APS `obs_delivery` and `narrative_frame` fields by well over a century.

Note that the 1886 printing exists in states with differing corrections; specify your printing if you cite page numbers.

`KEY` · `frame: method`

## Genre and transmission

### Honko, Lauri — "Memorates and the Study of Folk Beliefs"

*Journal of the Folklore Institute* 1, no. 1/2 (1964): 5–19. DOI [10.2307/3814027](https://doi.org/10.2307/3814027).

Introduces the **memorate** — a first-person account of a personally experienced supernatural encounter — as a genre distinct from the legend, which circulates in the third person and belongs to the community. This is the genre the APS collects, and naming it correctly matters: the archive holds memorates, and a memorate that has begun circulating as a legend has changed category and should be coded accordingly. The single most useful piece of vocabulary in this file.

`CORE` · `frame: method` · relates to `narrative_frame`

### Dégh, Linda, and Andrew Vázsonyi — "Does the Word 'Dog' Bite? Ostensive Action: A Problem in the Semiotics of Legend"

*Journal of Folklore Research* 20, no. 1 (1983): 5–34. ISSN 0737-7037. OCLC [742793593](https://www.worldcat.org/oclc/742793593).

Introduces **ostension**: the process by which legend migrates into action, so that people enact, test, or stage what a narrative describes. Operationally important because ostension contaminates collection — legend-tripping produces genuine experiences at sites whose reputation drew the witness there. Relevant to every 🗺️ Type VII site with a public reputation.

`KEY` · `frame: cultural` · cross-ref → 🚪 I, 🗺️ VII

### Primiano, Leonard Norman — "Vernacular Religion and the Search for Method in Religious Folklife"

*Western Folklore* 54, no. 1 (1995): 37–56. ISSN 0043-373X. OCLC [55605377](https://www.worldcat.org/oclc/55605377).

Introduces **vernacular religion** and argues against treating personal, improvised, non-institutional belief as a degraded version of doctrine. The methodological warrant for 🌐 Type IX: the witness's own framework is the data, not an error standing between the analyst and the data.

`CORE` · `frame: method` · cross-ref → 🌐 IX

### Blank, Trevor J., ed. — *Folklore and the Internet: Vernacular Expression in a Digital World*

Logan: Utah State University Press, 2009. ISBN 9780874217506. OCLC [317928046](https://www.worldcat.org/oclc/317928046).

Establishes networked space as a folkloric ecosystem with its own transmission pathways and folk groups. The methodological basis for treating digital-substrate reports as collectible rather than as noise — which is what the v4.1 decision to fold digital cases into 🚪 Type I rests on.

`KEY` · `frame: cultural` · cross-ref → 🚪 I

### Goldstein, Diane E., Sylvia Ann Grider, and Jeannie Banks Thomas — *Haunting Experiences: Ghosts in Contemporary Folklore*

Logan: Utah State University Press, 2007. ISBN 9780874216363. OCLC [145431719](https://www.worldcat.org/oclc/145431719).

Treats ghost narrative as social practice — legend-tripping, ghost tours, and televised investigation as meaning-making activities with their own rules. Useful as a corrective to the assumption that witnesses arrive without a media repertoire; most do not, and the repertoire shapes what gets reported and in what words.

`KEY` · `frame: cultural` · cross-ref → 👁 VI, 🗺️ VII

## Theoretical framing of anomaly

### Fort, Charles — *The Book of the Damned*

New York: Boni and Liveright, 1919. Reprint, New York: Tarcher, 2016. ISBN 9781101983249. OCLC [1061487](https://www.worldcat.org/oclc/1061487).

Fort catalogues reports that institutional science excluded, and attacks the exclusion itself rather than defending any explanation of the reports. Read as the origin of anomalistics as a stance — and as a caution: Fort's method was collection without analysis, which is the failure mode an archive without a schema arrives at.

`KEY` · `frame: anomalistic`

### Hansen, George P. — *The Trickster and the Paranormal*

New York: Xlibris, 2001. ISBN 9781401000813. OCLC [47238157](https://www.worldcat.org/oclc/47238157).

Hansen applies anthropological and structuralist theory — Turner on liminality, Lévi-Strauss and Hyde on the trickster — to argue that instability, ambiguity, and boundary-violation are intrinsic to high-strangeness reports rather than artefacts of poor data. Whatever one makes of the conclusion, the framework is the reason this sits under PROTOCOL rather than 🛸 Type III: it is an argument about why the evidence in *every* type behaves as it does, including why cases degrade when investigated.

**Moved from** `APS_Biblio_HighStrangeness` (v3.0), where it was filed by subject. Self-published; the scholarship it draws on is not.

`KEY` · `frame: method` · cross-ref → 🛸 III

## Research ethics

### Smith, Linda Tuhiwai — *Decolonizing Methodologies: Research and Indigenous Peoples*

3rd ed. London: Zed Books (Bloomsbury), 2021. ISBN 9781786998132 (pbk.). DOI [10.5040/9781350225282](https://doi.org/10.5040/9781350225282).
*Orig. ed.:* London: Zed Books; Dunedin: University of Otago Press, 1999. OCLC [39556459](https://www.worldcat.org/oclc/39556459).

Smith's account of research *as experienced by the researched* — the history of extraction, and what a community is owed by someone who arrives to collect its accounts. Directly load-bearing for the APS, which records `indigenous_territory` on every case and preserves witness framing as primary.

The project's existing privacy architecture handles consent at the level of the individual witness: `permission_level` governs publication, and `community_context` (column AO) is withheld from every public export because Q14 concerns third parties who have not consented. Smith's argument is that **individual consent does not exhaust the obligation** where the account belongs to a tradition as well as to a person. That gap is open, named in the 🌐 Type IX file, and not resolved by anything currently in the schema.

`CORE` · `frame: method` · cross-ref → 🌐 IX
