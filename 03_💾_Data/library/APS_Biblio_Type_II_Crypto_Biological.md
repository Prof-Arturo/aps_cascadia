## Scope

Encounters with an unknown biological entity: tracks, scat, vocalisations, feeding sign, locomotion, and behaviour that reads as that of an animal. Also the cultural history of the unknown animal, which in Cascadia is not separable from the reports.

## Boundary notes

**🐾 II vs 👁 VI** — the registry states the rule: *distinguished from Type VI by physical evidence.* An upright figure that leaves prints, breaks branches, is heard breathing, or displays foraging or avoidance behaviour is Type II. A figure that leaves nothing, appears and is gone, or behaves in a way the witness describes as non-biological is Type VI. When a witness reports both an entity and no trace, Type VI is primary with II secondary — not the reverse.

**🐾 II vs 🌐 IX** — where a witness names the entity within a specific cultural or tribal tradition, **Type IX is primary and Type II secondary.** The witness's framework governs. This is not a judgement about what was seen; it is a statement about which framework the record preserves as primary. Cascadia intake makes this live rather than theoretical, and the 🌐 Type IX file is under a consultation hold — code the case, flag `cultural_frame_flag`, and do not publish ahead of that consultation.

**Regional note.** Cascadia carries the densest Sasquatch report history in North America and the most developed local repertoire. Expect high ostension: witnesses frequently arrive already fluent. Record the fluency in `narrative_frame`; it is data about transmission, not a mark against the account.

## Reading order

**Buhs 2009** for the cultural history, **Naish 2017** for the zoological baseline, **Regal 2011** for how the field positioned itself. That order is deliberate — reading the advocacy literature first makes the historiography harder to see.

## History of the field

### Heuvelmans, Bernard — *On the Track of Unknown Animals*

Translated by Richard Garnett. New York: Hill & Wang, 1958. ISBN 9780800854081. [OpenLibrary](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL458770W/On_the_track_of_unknown_animals)

The founding text of modern cryptozoology, cataloguing claimed unknown animals to argue for a quasi-scientific investigative programme. Essential for understanding how the field framed itself as empirical rather than folkloric — a framing choice with consequences the discipline is still carrying.

`CORE` · `frame: anomalistic`

### Regal, Brian — *Searching for Sasquatch: Crackpots, Eggheads, and Cryptozoology*

New York: Palgrave Macmillan, 2011. ISBN 9780230109865. [Publisher](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1057/9780230118294)

Traces the scientific and subcultural history of Bigfoot research, and the mutual definition of academic and amateur positions across the twentieth century. The best account of how a question becomes disreputable, and of what that does to the people who keep asking it.

`CORE` · `frame: cultural`

### Buhs, Joshua Blu — *Bigfoot: The Life and Times of a Legend*

Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 2009. ISBN 9780226079896. [Publisher](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/B/bo6992635.html)

Treats Bigfoot as a cultural object, linking the legend to class, regional identity, and media economics. A social history rather than a defence or a debunking, which is the posture closest to the project's own. The chapters on the Pacific Northwest are directly relevant to Region 10.

`CORE` · `frame: cultural`

## Zoological assessment

### Naish, Darren — *Hunting Monsters: Cryptozoology and the Reality Behind the Myths*

London: Arcturus Publishing, 2017. ISBN 9781784285485. OCLC [995621935](https://www.worldcat.org/oclc/995621935).

A palaeontologist's assessment of cryptid claims by zoological plausibility and known patterns of misidentification. Held here as the mechanism baseline for the type: an analyst who cannot say what the ordinary explanations *are* cannot recognise a case that resists them. Naish reaches negative conclusions; methodological agnosticism means the archive carries the argument without adopting the verdict, exactly as it carries Heuvelmans without adopting his.

`CORE` · `frame: mechanism`

### O'Connor, Ralph — *The Earth on Show: Fossils and the Poetics of Popular Science, 1802–1856*

Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 2007. ISBN 9780226616671. [Publisher](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/E/bo5519175.html)

How early palaeontology was communicated to lay audiences, and how it built the expectation that prehistoric creatures might persist. Background rather than foreground, but it answers a question the other works assume: why *surviving relict population* became the culturally available explanation in the first place.

`KEY` · `frame: cultural`

## Removed from this bibliography

**Dendle, Peter.** "The Zombie as Barometer of Cultural Anxiety," in *Monsters and the Monstrous* (Rodopi, 2007) — carried in v3.0 on the grounds that its framework "extends to other type II monsters that embody collective fears." The framework does extend; the subject does not. No witness has filed a Type II report of a zombie, and the essay is about horror media rather than about reported encounter. Removed in v4.0 as outside the type's evidentiary scope. Buhs 2009 does the cultural-anxiety work with material the archive actually holds.

Logged in the Index under *Removed citations*.

## Gaps

- **Eyewitness identification of animals under poor conditions.** The misidentification literature is represented only through Naish's summary. Primary research on observer estimation of size, distance, and gait at low light would be directly usable in debriefing.
- **Regional wildlife baseline.** No Cascadia-specific source on bear, cougar, and elk behaviour, bipedal bear locomotion, or seasonal vocalisation. This is the most immediately practical gap in the file.
- **Indigenous accounts on their own terms.** Deliberately held pending the 🌐 Type IX consultation rather than filled unilaterally. See that file.
