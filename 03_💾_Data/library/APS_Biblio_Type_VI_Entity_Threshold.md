## Scope

Contact with a non-biological entity: figures seen at the edge of sleep or in full waking consciousness, shadow figures, ancestral visitation, presences felt without being seen — what the folkloric literature calls *the seen*.

**"Threshold" is both temporal and spatial.** Temporally, the hypnagogic and hypnopompic margins of sleep. Spatially, doorways, hallways, treelines, the edge of peripheral vision. Reports cluster at both kinds of edge, and the type is named for the pattern rather than for a setting.

## Boundary notes

**👁 VI vs 💭 IV** — the boundary the taxonomy most depends on. Type VI is a presence located **outside** the witness. Type IV is an impression located **within** them. A figure at the bedside is Type VI *even though* the witness was in bed and possibly between sleep and waking: the setting does not decide the type, the phenomenology does.

The 🛌 glyph was retired from Type IV in v4.1 for precisely this reason — a bedside icon on Type IV drew these cases away from Type VI. Do not reintroduce it.

**👁 VI vs 🐾 II** — physical evidence. Tracks, scat, vocalisation, foraging behaviour → Type II. A figure that leaves nothing → Type VI. Where a witness reports an entity *and* no trace, Type VI is primary.

**👁 VI vs 🌐 IX** — where the witness names the entity within a tradition — ancestor, saint, a named being from a specific cosmology — **Type IX is primary, VI secondary.** The naming is the witness's framework asserting itself, and the framework is what Type IX exists to preserve. This boundary carries the most ethical weight of any in the taxonomy; see the 🌐 Type IX file before publishing such a case.

**Felt presence without a figure** is Type VI, not Type VIII. Type VIII covers diffuse phenomena with no implied agent; a sense of *someone* implies an agent.

## Reading order

**Hufford 1995** ("Beings Without Bodies", in the Methodology file) first — it is the theoretical statement for this entire type. Then **Alderson-Day 2023**, then **Cheyne et al. 1999**.

## Core

### Alderson-Day, Ben — *Presence: The Strange Science and True Stories of the Unseen Other*

Manchester: Manchester University Press, 2023. ISBN 9781526173508 (hbk.). 304 pp.

The current synthesis on **felt presence** across its contexts: sleep paralysis, bereavement, endurance sport and high-altitude climbing, psychosis, sensory deprivation, and ordinary life. Alderson-Day is a psychologist working on inner speech and hallucination, and the book's organising argument is that felt presence is a common and structured human experience rather than a marginal or pathological one — which is the empirical claim Type VI's existence as a category rests on.

The most important single acquisition for this type. If the project buys one book for Type VI, this is it.

`CORE` · `frame: mechanism`

### Cheyne, J. Allan, Steve D. Rueffer, and Ian R. Newby-Clark — "Hypnagogic and Hypnopompic Hallucinations during Sleep Paralysis: Neurological and Cultural Construction of the Night-Mare"

*Consciousness and Cognition* 8, no. 3 (1999): 319–337.

The factor-analytic study that separates sleep-paralysis experience into three clusters: **Intruder** (sensed presence, fear, visual and auditory anomaly), **Incubus** (pressure, breathing difficulty, pain), and **Vestibular-Motor** (floating, flying, out-of-body sensation). Directly operational — the Intruder cluster is the Type VI presentation, the Vestibular-Motor cluster is Type IV, and the same witness can report both in one episode. The clearest available basis for coding a mixed bedside report into primary and secondary types rather than guessing.

A DOI for this article could not be confirmed; cite volume, issue and pages. The article's own title page capitalises and hyphenates "Night-Mare" as given above, though citing sources often render it "nightmare".

`CORE` · `frame: mechanism` · cross-ref → 💭 IV

### Luhrmann, T. M. — *When God Talks Back: Understanding the American Evangelical Relationship with God*

New York: Alfred A. Knopf, 2012. ISBN 9780307264794.

Luhrmann's ethnography of how evangelical practitioners learn, through disciplined practice, to experience a present and responsive other. The relevant finding for Type VI is that **the capacity to experience presence is trainable** — attention, expectation, and practice shape whether and how a presence is encountered. This complicates any analysis that treats presence reports as purely spontaneous, and it is a mechanism account that takes the experience entirely seriously.

`CORE` · `frame: cultural` · cross-ref → 🌐 IX, 💭 IV

## Gaps

This file is four citations deep and needs roughly three times that. Named in priority order:

1. **The apparition corpus.** Tyrrell's *Apparitions* (1943) and Green & McCreery's *Apparitions* (1975) are the standard case treatments and are absent.
2. **Bereavement and post-mortem contact.** Among the most commonly reported presence experiences anywhere, with a substantial clinical literature on its prevalence and on its being unrelated to pathology. Entirely unrepresented, and the most likely category of report the Type VI outreach will actually generate.
3. **Shadow-figure and peripheral-vision reports** as a distinct contemporary form, including its internet-mediated transmission.
4. **Charles Bonnet syndrome and visual-release hallucination** beyond Sacks's summary — the leading conventional account of seen figures in older witnesses, and necessary for responsible coding.
5. **Hypnagogia** as a research area in its own right.
6. **Historical and folkloric antecedents** — the Old Hag, the mara, nightmare traditions across cultures. Hufford covers Newfoundland; the comparative literature is not here.
