**Status:** ACTIVE REFERENCE  |  **Version:** 1.0

---

## How to use this document

Five pitches for five different rooms. They are not a script to memorize — they are the shape of the right answer, so that an improvised version lands in the same register.

The thing that goes wrong in live conversation is drift: a question gets asked in the paranormal-investigation register, and the answer follows the question's framing instead of the project's. Each pitch below is built to redirect without correcting the person asking.

**These are distinct from the Field Manual §6 elevator pitches**, which are scripts for securing permission to post a flyer in a café or community board. Different job, different room. Keep them apart so neither drifts into the other.

---

## 1. The ten-second version

*For: "So what do you do?" — a party, a trailhead, a waiting room.*

> I run a citizen science archive that collects anomalous experience stories across the Pacific Northwest. The things people see that they can't explain and usually never tell anyone. We don't debunk them and we don't prove them. We collect them carefully, and eventually we'll tell them.

**Why it works:** names the activity before the subject matter. "Citizen science archive" lands first, which sets the register before the listener's own associations can.

---

## 2. The standard thirty seconds

*For: the default. Anyone who asked a real question and is still listening.*

> The American Periphenomenal Survey is a storytelling project with a database attached. We collect accounts of anomalous experience from people living in the Cascadia bioregion — missing time, a figure at the edge of vision, a low hum nobody else can hear, a stretch of road where the same thing keeps happening to different people across forty years.
>
> Most people who have an experience like this never tell anyone. The ones who do usually get one of two responses: they're humored, or they're corrected. We do neither. We record the account in the witness's own words, code it against a nine-type taxonomy, and preserve it so it can be compared with every other account in the archive.
>
> The infrastructure exists to make the stories tellable. The data is the substrate. The stories are the work.

**Why it works:** the middle paragraph does the heavy lifting. "Humored or corrected" names the two failure modes the project exists between, which is faster than explaining methodological agnosticism from first principles.

## 3. For a journalist

*For: press, podcasts, anyone whose job is to find the hook.*

> There's a category of experience that almost everyone has brushed against and almost nobody reports. We're building the archive for it in the Pacific Northwest.
>
> What makes it unusual is the discipline: we don't decide what the experience was. If a witness says skinwalker, the record says skinwalker. If they say glitch in the Matrix, the record says that. Their framing is the data, not an error to correct. We're cartographers of the unknown, not ghost hunters.

**Why it works:** gives the hook in the first sentence so the interviewer doesn't have to hunt for one, then immediately supplies the discipline. The two concrete examples — one indigenous-framed, one internet-native — demonstrate the range without editorializing about either.

**Watch for:** a journalist who paraphrases "cartographers of the unknown." Paraphrased taglines drift toward the paranormal-investigation register. Ask for it quoted verbatim or omitted.

---

## 4. For an academic or regional partner

*For: folklorists, anthropologists, prospective regional unit managers, anyone evaluating rigor.*

> The APS sits at the intersection of three fields without belonging to any of them: anomalistics, humanistic psychology, and experience-centered folklore in the Hufford tradition.
>
> Methodologically we're descended from two things — the Grimms' commitment to linguistic fidelity, recording the vernacular exactly as spoken, and the SPR's 1889 Census of Hallucinations, which found pattern through scale rather than through any single case. We've built a nine-type taxonomy with cross-cutting attributes for substrate, witness configuration, and narrative frame, so co-occurrence across types becomes measurable.
>
> It's an unusual opportunity: most folklore scholarship works backward from established tradition. We're positioned to watch vernacular narrative form in real time.

**Why it works:** names the lineage before the apparatus. An academic audience evaluates whether you know the literature before they evaluate what you built.

**Follow-up to expect:** "What's your sample size?" See the honesty note below.

## 5. For the skeptical question

*For: "So you believe in this stuff?" — the most common question you will get.*

> That's the one question the project is built to not answer. Belief and disbelief are both ontological commitments, and either one corrupts the collection.
>
> What I'll say is that the accounts are real in the sense that matters: real people had real experiences and described them consistently, across cultures and across centuries. Whether a given one turns out to be sleep paralysis or infrasound or something we don't have a name for yet — that's a question for later, and for somebody with better instruments than mine. My job is to make sure the account still exists when they get here.

**Why it works:** refuses the frame without refusing the person. Naming sleep paralysis and infrasound as live possibilities signals that the project is not credulous, which is what the question is actually testing for.

## Honesty note — archive size

**None of these pitches cite numbers, deliberately.**

The archive is early. An honest answer is better than a framing that implies scale the archive does not yet have, and a journalist or academic who asks and gets a number that lands differently than the pitch implied will discount everything else.

When it comes up:

> Early, and growing. Cascadia launched first because it's home, and the framework scales to ten bioregions.

Do not inflate. Do not apologize for the size either — a carefully built archive of a few dozen well-coded cases is a more serious object than a few thousand uncoded ones, and that is worth saying plainly if pressed.

## Phrases to avoid when improvising

The pitches above are safe. Improvisation is where the register slips. Avoid:

- *paranormal*, *supernatural*, *occult* — ontological claims the project does not make
- *investigate*, *investigation* — we document; investigation implies a verdict is coming
- *believer* / *skeptic* — both are positions the project declines
- *proof*, *evidence that* — the archive is not building a case
- *subject*, *respondent* — the person is a **witness**
- Any sentence beginning *"What really happened was…"*

See `voice_style_guide.md` §3 for the full terminology swap table and §5 for the three prohibited speech acts.
