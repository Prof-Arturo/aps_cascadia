# The Place Where Things Keep Happening

*A public explainer from the American Periphenomenal Survey*

---

Most anomalous experiences are single events: something happened to one person, once, and never again. But there is another category of report, older than any of us and more stubborn than any single account, and it is a category the American Periphenomenal Survey is only just beginning to properly document.

It is the *place* where things happen.

A stretch of road where drivers keep reporting the same figure. A house where three families in a row report the same footsteps. A trail where hikers over four decades describe the same feeling of being watched, the same wrong turn, the same clearing that is not on any map. A neighborhood where the same story surfaces in the newspaper archive every fifteen years, under different names, with the same shape.

Some places are stories. Some places tell the same story to everyone who listens.

## What we are collecting

Locations across the Cascadia Bioregion where anomalous experiences occur to more than one person, across time. This is a different kind of report from a single event. We are asking not "did something happen to you" but "is there a place — a road, a house, a trail, a room, a stretch of ground — where things keep happening, to different people, over time."

The kinds of pattern that qualify:

- **The same phenomenon, different witnesses** — a location where multiple people, unknown to each other, describe similar experiences. The similarity is what interests us, especially when the witnesses did not know about each other's accounts.
- **The story that keeps coming back** — a location with a durable reputation in a family, a neighborhood, a workplace, or a community. Not the tellings themselves, but the *place* the tellings return to.
- **The place locals warn about** — a spot people who live in the area know to be careful around, often without being able to say exactly why, sometimes across generations.
- **The location with a written record** — a place that turns up in the newspaper archive, in local histories, on old maps annotated in ways nobody remembers annotating, or in the oral tradition of the people who lived there before the current residents.

## Why we collect them

The idea that some places are anomalous in themselves — independent of any particular event or witness — is one of the oldest ideas in the folklore record. It goes by different names in different traditions: sacred sites, *genius loci*, window areas, thin places, feared crossings, places-where-the-veil-is-thin. Every culture that has been documented has a version of this concept.

Contemporary research has been slow to take these locations seriously as *locations* rather than as backdrops for individual events. When a researcher studies "the ghost at Bridge X," the study is usually about the ghost, not the bridge. But there is a growing case — from folklorists, from parapsychologists working on so-called "window areas," from indigenous scholars — that some locations reliably produce anomalous experiences across witnesses and time in ways that resist reduction to any single cause.

We are trying to build a proper archive of these places in Cascadia. Not to prove they are haunted — we do not use that word, and we make no ontological claims about what any location is or is not. To document what has been reported at these locations, by whom, when, under what conditions, and whether the accounts have anything structurally in common.

This kind of archive is longitudinal by definition. A Type VII case is not a single event; it is a record that grows as more reports arrive about the same place. Over years, the pattern (or its absence) becomes visible.

## What we are not collecting

**We are not collecting tourist attractions.** Locations that have been packaged as "haunted destinations" — with tours, plaques, gift shops, or paid ghost-hunting sessions — are almost always contaminated as data. The reports at those locations have been shaped by the marketing and by each other. We may still hear about them, and we may still record what we hear, but we treat them with appropriate caution.

**We are not collecting rumors.** "I heard someone say that place is haunted" is not what we are looking for. What we are looking for is: you experienced something specific there, or you know of specific accounts from specific people who did.

**We are not collecting locations we cannot name.** Type VII cases require enough locational specificity that another researcher could, in principle, visit the site. Vague geographic descriptions ("somewhere in the Cascades") are recorded but rarely useful.

## What happens when you submit

You describe the location, the reports you know of, and (if applicable) your own experience there. The location is added to the Cascadia Archive as a Type VII candidate site. It will not be considered a confirmed Type VII case until multiple independent reports have accumulated over time.

We do not publish location details in the public registry without careful review. Locations that appear on private property, sensitive sites (indigenous sacred sites, active memorial sites, sites with public-safety implications), or locations that could attract disruptive visitors are held in a private tier of the archive and not published.

## Submit

If you know of a place where things keep happening — one you have been to, one your family talks about, one the local paper has covered more than once — we would like to hear about it.
