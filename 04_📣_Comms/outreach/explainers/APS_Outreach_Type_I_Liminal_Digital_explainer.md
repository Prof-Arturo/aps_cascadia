# When Space and Time Feel Wrong

*A public explainer from the American Periphenomenal Survey*

---

Most of us can point to at least one experience — often just a moment, sometimes an entire afternoon — that did not fit. The road you drive every day looped when it should not have. An hour disappeared and you cannot say where it went. A hallway seemed longer walking in than walking back out. A stretch of trail repeated itself. You picked up your phone and the first thing on the screen was the exact thing you had just been thinking about, unsearched, unspoken.

These experiences are not rare. They are among the most commonly reported anomalous experiences in the world, across every culture that has been surveyed, in every era on record. Most people who have one do not talk about it. Some file it away as a strange story to tell close friends. Some spend years quietly wondering what happened. Very few are ever asked to describe it in enough detail to make sense of it.

The American Periphenomenal Survey collects these accounts.

## What we are collecting

Reports of moments when the ordinary structure of space, time, place, or the digital environment briefly did not behave the way it should. This includes:

- **Missing time** — a period you cannot account for, from a few minutes to hours, without an explanation like sleep or intoxication that would cover it.
- **Places that repeat, loop, or fail to end** — a familiar road, hallway, trail, or room that behaved wrongly for a stretch and then behaved normally again.
- **Geographies that briefly did not match** — a room in your own house that seemed longer, shorter, or wrong in a way you noticed at the time.
- **Unnatural silences** — a sudden and total dropout of ambient sound, often lasting seconds, sometimes preceding something else worth noting.
- **Digital environments that responded to unspoken thought** — a feed, search result, or algorithm that anticipated a question you had not typed, a memory you had not shared, or a name you had not spoken out loud.

The last category is the newest and the one we are most actively trying to hear about. It is also the one people are most reluctant to report, because the language for it is still being invented.

## Why we collect them

These reports have been collected in one form or another for as long as there have been researchers willing to listen — William James in the 1890s, the Society for Psychical Research at scale, folklorists like David Hufford working case by case for decades. What has not existed, until recently, is a project that treats analog experiences (the road that looped) and digital experiences (the feed that seemed to read your mind) as belonging to the same family.

We think they do. Both are moments when a person's inner attention and the world outside it briefly aligned in ways that resist ordinary explanation. The substrate is different — a forest trail is not a phone screen — but the phenomenology, the *experience of it happening*, may be closer than the difference in substrate suggests.

We are not trying to prove that these experiences are supernatural. We are not trying to explain them away either. We are trying to collect enough of them, described carefully enough by the people who had them, to see what patterns emerge across witnesses, regions, and time.

## What happens when you submit

Your account is added to the Cascadia Archive as a case file. It is coded by an analyst — location, timing, the phenomenology as you described it, whether there were other witnesses, whether it resembles other reports on file. Your contact information is kept separate from your account and never published.

You choose, at the time of submission, whether your account may appear in the public registry (with all identifying information removed) or is held for internal research only. This choice is yours; it is confirmed at the end of the interview if we speak with you directly; you can change your mind later.

If your account is unusual enough or connects to an ongoing area of research, we may ask if we can follow up with a longer conversation. There is no obligation to say yes. Most submissions are received, coded, and added to the archive without further contact.

## Submit

If any of this describes something you experienced — recently, or years ago — we would like to hear from you.
