# The Sound No One Else Hears

*A public explainer from the American Periphenomenal Survey*

---

You hear a low humming. It has been in the background for weeks, or months, or as long as you can remember it being one particular way. You have described it to your family, or your partner, or your doctor, and they say they cannot hear it. Sometimes they suggest it is your imagination. Sometimes they suggest it is your ears. You are not sure anymore.

You feel a pressure in a specific room of a house that no one else feels. You watch electronics fail repeatedly in the same location and nowhere else. You see a light over the water that is not any known aircraft, not any known ground source, and not something anyone with you noticed. You experience cold in a stable environment and the thermostat says nothing has changed.

You are not alone. These reports are among the most consistent and most widely distributed anomalous experiences in the world. They are also among the most frequently dismissed, because the phenomena themselves are — by their nature — diffuse, non-visible, and difficult to record. What you are noticing may not be visible. That does not mean it is not there.

The American Periphenomenal Survey collects these accounts.

## What we are collecting

Reports of anomalous energetic and environmental phenomena — perceptual or measurable events not attributable to identified meteorological, geological, or technological sources. This includes:

- **The Hum** — a persistent, low-frequency sound (often described as a diesel engine idling in the distance, or a refrigerator with no refrigerator) that comes and goes, is heard by some people in a location but not others, and does not correspond to any identified sound source. Cascadia has documented Hum reports going back decades. If you hear it, we want to know when, where, and how.
- **Pressure or presence in the air** — a felt heaviness, charge, or change in the "feel" of a room, corridor, or outdoor location, without a visible source. Sometimes described as a barometric-pressure sensation, sometimes as something else the witness has no word for.
- **Unexplained lights** — visible lights over land, water, or sky that do not correspond to known aircraft, satellites, ground vehicles, weather phenomena, or reflections. The Marfa lights in Texas are the most famous case; Cascadia has its own quieter equivalents.
- **Electromagnetic strangeness** — electronics failing, batteries draining, compasses drifting, watches stopping, cameras malfunctioning — in a repeatable location. What makes this different from ordinary equipment failure is the location-specificity: the same devices fail in the same place, and work normally elsewhere.
- **Temperature or air anomalies** — cold zones or warm zones in a stable environment, felt clearly by the witness, without a mechanical cause.
- **Persistent geophysical strangeness** — anything else in this category we have not thought of yet, that occurs in the ambient environment rather than to a specific witness.

## Why we collect them

Reports in this category share an important feature that makes them methodologically distinctive: they are often felt by *some* people in a location and not others, they persist over time, and they resist a satisfying single-cause explanation. The Hum in particular has been studied by acoustical engineers, geologists, and psychologists for over four decades, and the mainstream scientific literature has settled on nothing more definitive than "some people hear it and we do not yet know why."

That is not a failure of the reports. That is a description of an interesting phenomenon that mainstream tools are only beginning to be adequate to.

Our approach is to collect a large enough number of well-described accounts, from a large enough geographic area, that patterns can emerge over time. When did the reports start? Do they cluster geographically? Do they correlate with any known environmental variables — geological, industrial, hydrological, electromagnetic? Do the same witnesses report multiple phenomena in this category? Do the same locations produce reports across time and across unrelated witnesses?

These questions cannot be answered by any single report. They can be answered — or at least approached — by an archive.

## A note on witnesses who feel alone

Type VIII experiences are unusual in one specific way: witnesses often feel unusually isolated. Someone who saw a strange light in the sky can typically find others who saw it too. Someone who hears the Hum in a room where others cannot often feels — sometimes for years — that they are the only one experiencing what they are experiencing.

You are not the only one. If you have looked for online communities around a phenomenon like this, you have likely found some — the World Hum Map, EMF sensitivity forums, and so on. Those communities are real and often useful. What they are not is a research archive. The APS is trying to build one, and part of that work is providing a place where the report is taken seriously as data, without either being dismissed or absorbed into a specific ontological framework.

## What happens when you submit

Your account is added to the Cascadia Archive. It is coded by an analyst according to the phenomenon type (Hum, pressure, lights, EM, temperature, or other), the location (as specifically as you are willing to share), the duration and pattern of onset, whether other witnesses were present, and any environmental context we can capture. Your contact information is kept separate and never published.

You choose whether your account appears in the public registry (identifying information removed) or is held for internal research only. This is your decision.

For sustained phenomena like the Hum, we are particularly interested in longitudinal data — when it started, when it changes, when it stops. If you have kept notes over time, we would like to hear from you.

## Submit

If you have been hearing, feeling, or seeing something no one else does — for a week, or for years — we would like to hear from you.
